06. Interviewer – for the very first time

First time being an interviewer and lessons learned about suitability, honesty, and communication.

Ngan Ho · April 12, 2024 · 4 min read

My company had an intern recruitment period recently, and this is the very first time I joined an interview as the interviewer. At the beginning, I was like, “Oh, can I actually do it? If my English isn't good enough compared to the interviewee, will they judge me? haha.” But then everything went very smoothly, and there are a lot of things I learned after that “first time”:

(Update from 2026: The candidates I interviewed and chose at this time turned out to be so good that both of them became my boss's favorites afterward 😄)

Suitability

An interview is not only for the interviewer to evaluate a potential candidate, but it works both ways—both sides need to find a suitable match. Not only does the company want to find someone who has the required skills for the position, but the candidate also needs to see if the company has an environment that can foster their potential and match their career orientation.

This clarity right from the beginning helps both sides understand expectations and adjust accordingly. For context, my company's field is in Supply Chain (SC) Analytics and Modeling, which is a quite niche sector in Vietnam with very few companies operating in it.

I once heard a Supply Chain student from an quite-good university say that when they graduate, they have two main directions: one is becoming a Demand Planner in a big corporation, and the other is becoming an Analyst at my company. Sound cool, right?

But working in consulting isn't always a bed of roses. My boss and I know very well that this environment isn't just a dream landscape; there will be weeks where we work 12-14 hours a day just to meet deadlines and finalize our data analysis. Therefore, being clear and transparent from the start is the best choice we can make for the candidates.

Honesty

This links to a characteristic of my boss that I really admire: “Honesty”. My boss once told me:

“You know the reason why you’re here, right? Because everyday we go to work, we do the things that we believe it’s meaningful and we add value to the others. To be honest, if the first priority of someone is money, our company is not the best place for them. We try to pay the best we can, but compared to other corporations, for sure we’re not the top player”

And the part about “We try the best we can” is incredibly honest and respectable in my perspective. It helps me see someone's genuine intent and effort, even when the immediate reality might not completely match high expectations.

Other Learnings

Language proficiency and communication skills influence each other, but they are not the same thing:

  • The interviews at our company are conducted in English because once you join, communicating with the managers requires English.
  • However, I met a candidate from an unnamed university who got quite confused and flustered speaking English—let alone trying to explain concepts to an international manager.
  • I initially felt we might have to pass on him because he would feel overwhelmed entering the company, despite having good professional knowledge and relevant experience.
  • When my boss listened to my report, he gave me a great piece of advice: Next time, you can switch to speaking Vietnamese to check their actual communication skills. Language proficiency itself is something that can absolutely be improved over time, especially at an entry level like an internship, if they are truly a high-potential candidate.

The questions a candidate asks can reveal their way of thinking and how much they care about the position:

  • This is very clear, hehe. When you go to an interview, you should try to ask questions at the end of the session—no matter what it is, even if you feel the answer is obvious, do your best to ask.

Deep discussions with the candidate may not always be strictly necessary because interview time is limited. However, if time permits, it is highly beneficial for both sides to share how they structure their thoughts:

  • For example, during an interview, a candidate and I discussed his view on finance—he felt it was a bit boring because it's all about numbers and numbers.
  • But my boss noted that, at the end of the day, when we work on consulting projects, everything we do eventually needs to be translated into "money" - specifically, how much financial value or savings we are bringing to the client.
  • Then, I chimed in and added that Finance in Supply Chain is much less boring because it links directly to the physical flow of products. For instance, how much a product costs to sell, how much it costs to store that specific pallet inside the warehouse, or how much it costs to deliver it to customers. This context makes the numbers much easier to visualize and ties directly into the real-world operational picture of the business. My boss, who is not-easy-to-convinced seemed quite pleased with my addition, haha. It's not every day that my boss says “Good” to me without adding any critical notes :)))
