08. Bottom-up and Top-down in Project Management

Pros and Cons of the Top-down and Bottom-up methods in project planning.

Ngan Ho · November 26, 2024 · 5 min read

I recently finished the most complicated project in my 3 years of working in the consulting field.

One of the lessons I learned from this project is how to combine two approaches to project timeline management: the Top-down and Bottom-up methods. I am taking note of the pros and cons of these two planning approaches here to save this lesson for myself in future projects.

What are the Top-down and Bottom-up approaches?

Simply put, when you start project planning, there are two main ways to calculate the required timeline and resources:

Top-down approach:

  • You start from the methodology or framework you intend to advise the client on, and then you estimate how much total time you will need to execute that methodology.
  • This estimation step is subjective, relying heavily on a manager's past experience with similar projects plus an estimated buffer time for custom requirements unique to each client company.
  • For example, based on experience, you project that a new engagement will take roughly 9 months to finish, and you build the baseline project timeline entirely around that 9-month estimation.

Bottom-up approach:

  • Similar to top-down, you still begin with the solution or methodology suggested to solve the client's problem.
  • The key difference is that from there, you break down that methodology into distinct, granular tasks. You then estimate the timeframe for each individual line item and add them up to find the final project duration.
  • For example, when planning a supply chain project that utilizes data to identify an optimal network for a client, we break down the approach into 4 main core tasks: data cleaning, data analysis to report on current network performance, data modelling to build the mathematical optimization model for the network, and finally delivering actionable recommendations for the business to execute.
  • The total time allocated to each small task is aggregated together, showing that we ultimately need 12 months in total for all sequential work items.

What are the advantages and disadvantages of each method?

The advantages of the Top-down method are:

  • You can quickly map out your project plan and maintain greater flexibility regarding specific execution steps. The vast majority of consulting projects are "explore-along-the-way" - meaning that while the plan looks one way initially, getting the actual data often reveals new insights, shifting the overall methodology and problem-solving direction.
  • Additionally, when negotiating a consulting project, your counterparts are usually at the manager level or C-level. They naturally focus on the big picture rather than minute technical complexities.
  • Therefore, if you can present a high-level vision and quickly conceptualize it into a clean project methodology for a proposal, you will connect better with the client and can secure their approval much faster.

What about the disadvantages of the Top-down approach?

  • Because ideas and timelines are quantified only at a high level, what happens if the combined effort of all tasks ends up larger than the total timeline allowed? You have to run multiple workstreams in parallel and bring in extra support during peak project phases.
  • Taking the exact same example from above, this results in a project timeline constraint where the true sum of the 4 core phases requires 12 months of effort, but the overall project timeline is strictly capped at 9 months.

Conversely, with the Bottom-up method:

  • Because you plan every single micro-task from the ground up, the probability of burning through your timeline or missing deadlines is significantly lower than with a Top-down approach.
  • However, if you spend too much time over-calculating tiny structural steps, you risk losing sight of the client's overarching big picture.
  • Consequently, communicating with management-level clients becomes far less effective; what you say might be incredibly accurate from a technical standpoint, but executives may not care about those granular details at all.

What is the solution to this?

Usually, a harmonious balance is achieved when two people—one with a top-down perspective and one with a bottom-up view—collaborate on a project proposal. The person with the top-down perspective establishes the high-level visionary framework. Based on that, the bottom-up thinker provides technical validation and adjusts the timeline realistically, preventing the project plan from becoming completely detached from practical constraints.

If you ask me which type of person I prefer I am, I would say I would like to be somewhere in the middle.

  • I started my career as an analyst, handling very detailed data tasks, so I lean naturally toward a bottom-up mindset.
  • But as I gain opportunities to step into higher roles—like currently, where I translate high-level requirements from clients and project managers into structured technical tasks for the team - I am learning to adopt a top-down perspective.
  • However, I am still working on sharpening my ability to conceptualize complex problems quickly. That means when a client explains their business issues, I need to translate that business challenge into mathematical or optimization problems to propose the right solution.
  • That is a core skill set I am eager to develop further in the future. But it's okay, there is still a long journey ahead. Improving by just 1% every single day is plenty.
Balancing both methods is far better than relying on just one. June 10, 2024
