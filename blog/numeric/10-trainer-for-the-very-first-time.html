10. Trainer – for the very first time

Lessons learned from my first time as a Trainer, presenting on Supply Chain Decarbonization to over 300 students.

Ngan Ho · April 7, 2025 · 6 min read

My company was the content sponsor for a student Supply Chain competition at an university. Due to a few unexpected shifts, I ended up stepping into the role of trainer to introduce and guide the technical knowledge for Round 1 of this year's competition.

My workshop topic was “Supply Chain Decarbonization”, which basically means reducing carbon emissions across end-to-end supply chain operations.

I absolutely loved this experience. When it comes to sharing, I prefer to share structured knowledge in a clear, lecture-style format that brings real value and insights to everyone, rather than just talking vaguely about my personal experiences or feelings.

Since this was my first time speaking in front of a fairly large crowd (3xx people), I've summarized what I learned from the training session into 3 key takeaways:

Tip #1: Keep slides minimalist, but keep your speech maximal

One skill I've picked up from working in management consulting is how to build beautiful slide decks.

Back in my university days, I used to design slides in a very standard academic format: cramming every piece of text onto the slide and then essentially reading it out loud during the presentation.

However, ever since joining my company and seeing how skillful my managers build slide decks — it's absolutely mesmerizing — I've tried my best to replicate that level of quality. The style I love most is conceptual and minimalist, meaning the slide contains only supporting graphics and as little text as possible, like this slide below:

The actual content covered by this single slide included definitions and case examples of:

  • The structural components that make up a supply chain
  • The ultimate rule: Everything starts with demand
  • The Bullwhip effect
  • Push and pull processes

But honestly, if you only looked at the slide alone, I challenge you to guess or understand any of that content, haha. As a people from Country X (famous for artistic lifestyle) would call it: that's art. An art of forcing your audience to actually focus on your speech instead of skipping class to just read the downloaded slides later. 😀

This fun little trick has been noted, in case I would become lecturer in the future!

Tip #3: ChatGPT did help, but the speaker's own baseline knowledge remains paramount

The absolute longest segment of the event was the Q&A session. The whole workshop ran for nearly 3 hours, and almost 2 hours of that was spent with me fielding questions from the crowd. The students showed deep interest in a highly diverse range of supply chain topics:

  1. From very core supply chain fundamentals, like: “Where is the push-pull decoupling point, and how do you differentiate between MTS, MTO, ATO, and ETO fulfillment models?”
  2. To trickier, yet still theoretical questions, such as: “How can we ensure data transparency in the carbon credit trading market?” or “How do we align multiple distinct entities across a shared supply chain to commit to the same decarbonization goals?”
  3. All the way to highly complex, macroeconomic, high-level questions where a definitive "correct" answer doesn't even exist yet, like: “How will the 46% tariff that Trump slapped on Vietnam just this morning impact supply chains broadly and green businesses specifically?”

Somehow, I managed to answer almost all of them, except for some questions that required follow up later because time is running out.

One of my absolute lifesavers was my colleague sitting right next to me, typing queries into ChatGPT :)))) For the straightforward structural questions like categories 1 and 2 above, ChatGPT would quickly spit out high-level concepts, and I would instantly wrap real-world case examples around them to form a complete, polished response.

For example, regarding the question on how to improve transparency in the carbon credit market, ChatGPT suggested 3 main pillars:

  • Standardization
  • Auditable reports
  • IoT tracking networks

I quickly paraphrased and expanded on that on the fly:

  • We can utilize global standardized frameworks like the GHG Protocol to ensure consistent measurement metrics across all domestic firms.
  • We need a legal framework that requires strict emissions auditing before any firm can actively trade on a carbon credit exchange — which I explained would operate very similarly to how public stock markets are governed.
  • And finally, implementing standardized physical IoT measurement devices ensures that data remains uniform across the system — like placing standardized carbon emission tracking meters on line-haul logistics trucks.
Basically, something along those lines.

As for that 3rd macroeconomic question, I was incredibly grateful that I had been actively keeping up with articles on the topic, since Trump took office up to the present. What caught me off guard was my initial belief in the predictions that Trump wouldn't target Vietnam so early, since Vietnam is a smaller market and he would want to take big swings at massive targets like China, the EU, or Canada first to gain leverage. But then again, if Trump were that predictable, he wouldn't be Trump, would he?

So, how did I address this massive question? I summarized my thoughts into 3 core angles:

  • The 46% figure might not be the final landing zone, as Vietnam has already dispatched a delegation to the US to actively negotiate before the tariffs officially take effect. The final percentage could be reduced, but it likely won't return to baseline levels. In the short term, Vietnamese export-driven enterprises will certainly face major headwinds.
  • The second angle came from a point made by our Partner. He noted that this situation presents a strategic window for Vietnamese enterprises to diversify their export destinations — shifting focus toward markets like the EU, which isn't threatening massive immediate tariffs and places a premium on highly sustainable, eco-friendly products.
  • Last but not least, if these steep pricing shifts begin heavily impacting the cost of living for everyday American consumers — since many imported products cannot be easily onshore-manufactured within the US (a fun example being durians) — we might see some rapid, unexpected policy turnarounds.

And I wrapped it up with a diplomatic, open-ended conclusion “The true beauty of the future lies in its uncertainty. When there are so many shifting scenarios that could play out, the best we can do is optimize our performance at each given phase :D”

All in all, the session went incredibly smoothly. Wrapping up the event at 9:30 PM with more than 2xx students still sitting in their seats actively listening felt like a massive win.

And that's that, until next time. April 7, 2025
